Problem 1 (:1):
Evaluate the improper integral ∫[1,∞] (1/x^4) dx. Which of the following is true?
A) The integral diverges.
B) The integral converges to a finite value.
C) The integral evaluates to 0.
D) The integral evaluates to infinity.
Correct Answer: B

Solution:
∫[1,∞] (1/x^4) dx = ∫[1,∞] x^(-4) dx. To determine convergence or divergence, we look at the limit as x approaches infinity of the integrand raised to a positive power: limx→∞(x^(-4))^(1/(-4)) = limx→∞ x^(-1) = 0. Since the limit is finite and non-zero, the integral converges.

Explanation:
The p-series test states that ∫[1,∞] x^(-p) dx converges if p > 1 and diverges if p ≤ 1. In this case, p = 4 > 1, so the integral converges.

Common mistake note:
A common mistake is to assume that because the integrand approaches zero as x increases, the integral must converge. However, it's the limit of the integrand raised to a positive power that determines convergence.

Problem 2 (:2):
Evaluate the improper integral ∫[1,∞] (1/x^3) dx. Which answer choice correctly describes its convergence status?
A) Converges to a finite value.
B) Diverges to infinity.
C) Converges to 0.
D) Converges to -infinity.
Correct Answer: B

Solution:
∫[1,∞] (1/x^3) dx = ∫[1,∞] x^(-3) dx. The limit as x approaches infinity of the integrand raised to a positive power is limx→∞(x^(-3))^(1/(-3)) = limx→∞ x^(-1) = ∞. Since the limit is infinite, the integral diverges.

Explanation:
The p-series test states that ∫[1,∞] x^(-p) dx converges if p > 1 and diverges if p ≤ 1. In this case, p = 3 > 1, but the limit of the integrand raised to a positive power is infinite, so the integral diverges.

Common mistake note:
A common mistake is to confuse the limit of the integrand with the convergence status. The limit being infinity does not imply that the integral diverges; it's the limit of the integrand raised to a positive power that matters.

Problem 3 (:3):
Evaluate the improper integral ∫[1,∞] (1/x^2) dx. Which answer choice is correct?
A) The integral converges to 0.
B) The integral diverges to infinity.
C) The integral converges to π/2.
D) The integral evaluates to a non-integer rational number.
Correct Answer: C

Solution:
∫[1,∞] (1/x^2) dx = ∫[1,∞] x^(-2) dx. This is the integral of 1/(x^2), which is the derivative of -1/x. Integrating from 1 to infinity gives us [-1/x]|_1^∞ = -1/∞ + 1 = 1.

Explanation:
The integral ∫[1,∞] x^(-2) dx can be evaluated by recognizing that it's the antiderivative of -1/x. The limit as x approaches infinity is 0, so the definite integral evaluates to a finite value.

Common mistake note:
A common mistake is to think that since x^(-2) approaches zero as x increases, the integral must converge to zero. However, the antiderivative of x^(-2) involves a negative sign, which changes the result.

A) The integral converges to π/3.
B) The integral diverges to infinity.
C) The integral evaluates to a non-integer rational number.
D) The integral converges to a finite value.
Correct Answer: D

Solution:
∫[1,∞] (1/x^6) dx = ∫[1,∞] x^(-6) dx. This is the integral of 1/(x^6), which is the derivative of -1/(5x^5). Integrating from 1 to infinity gives us [-1/(5x^5)]|_1^∞ = 0 - (-1/5) = 1/5.

Explanation:
The integral ∫[1,∞] x^(-6) dx can be evaluated by recognizing that it's the antiderivative of -1/(5x^5). The limit as x approaches infinity is 0, so the definite integral evaluates to a finite value.

Common mistake note:
A common mistake is to think that since x^(-6) approaches zero as x increases, the integral must converge to zero. However, the antiderivative involves a constant multiplier, which changes the result.
