Problem 1 (One-dimensional steady-state heat conduction in a rod):
A metal rod of length 1 meter and thermal conductivity k is heated at one end and kept at constant temperature T1 while the other end is kept at constant temperature T2. Which equation describes the temperature distribution along the rod?
A) T(x) = (T1 - T2) * x + T2
B) T(x) = T1 * e^(-kx)
C) T(x) = T1 + (T2 - T1) * x / L
D) T(x) = T1 * sin(kx)
Correct Answer: C

Solution:
The temperature distribution in a one-dimensional steady-state heat conduction problem is given by Fourier's law: T(x) = T1 + (T2 - T1) * x / L, where L is the length of the rod. Choice A represents a linear temperature increase but does not account for the length of the rod. Choice B represents an exponential decay which is not typical in steady-state conditions. Choice D represents a sinusoidal distribution, which is also not applicable to this problem.

Explanation:
This question assesses understanding of one-dimensional heat conduction and application of Fourier's law to find temperature distribution in a metal rod.

Common mistake note:
Common mistake: Choosing A or B because they represent simple linear or exponential functions, which are not accurate for steady-state heat conduction.

Problem 2 (Torque on a shaft):
A torque of 10 Nm is applied to a shaft with a radius of 5 cm. Assuming no other torques act on the shaft and it has a moment of inertia I = (1/2) * m * r^2, where m is mass and r is radius, what is the angular acceleration in rad/s^2?
A) 4
B) 8
C) 16
D) 32
Correct Answer: A

Solution:
The torque τ = Iα, where α is the angular acceleration. Given τ = 10 Nm and I = (1/2) * m * r^2 = (1/2) * m * (0.05 m)^2, we can solve for α: 10 = (1/2) * m * (0.05)^2 * α. Since the mass is not given and does not affect the ratio τ/I, we can ignore it. Solving for α gives α = 4 rad/s^2.

Explanation:
This question tests understanding of torque and angular acceleration relationships in an engineering context.

Common mistake note:
Common mistake: Including mass (m) in the calculation, which is unnecessary since it cancels out or assuming a constant moment of inertia without considering the radius.

Problem 3 (Bridge truss analysis):
A bridge truss designed with triangular elements to distribute load evenly. In a Warren truss with 6 vertical members and 5 horizontal members, how many triangles are formed?
A) 9
B) 10
C) 11
D) 12
Correct Answer: B

Solution:
In a Warren truss, each horizontal member forms a triangle with the two vertical members it connects. With 5 horizontal members and 6 vertical members, we have 5 triangles formed by the first horizontal member, 4 more by the second, 3 by the third, 2 by the fourth, and 1 by the fifth. Summing these gives 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10 triangles.

Explanation:
This question tests ability to analyze truss structures and count the number of triangles formed in an engineering context.

Common mistake note:
Common mistake: Counting only the triangles formed by one set of horizontal members, forgetting that each additional member adds fewer new triangles.

Problem 4 (Damped harmonic oscillator):
A damped harmonic oscillator has a damping coefficient c and natural frequency ω_n. The equation of motion is m * d^2x/dt^2 + c * dx/dt + k * x = 0, where m is the mass and k is the spring constant. Which term represents energy dissipation in the system?
A) m * d^2x/dt^2
B) c * dx/dt
C) k * x
D) All terms contribute
Correct Answer: B

Solution:
The term c * dx/dt represents damping or energy dissipation in the system. It is proportional to the velocity of the oscillator and causes a decrease in the system's total energy over time.

Explanation:
This question tests understanding of damped harmonic oscillators and role of damping in energy dissipation.

Common mistake note:
Common mistake: Choosing A, which represents the kinetic energy term; C, which represents the potential energy term; or D, assuming all terms contribute to energy dissipation equally.

Problem 5 (Stress-strain relationship):
A material subjected to a tensile force of 500 N and experiences an extension of 2 mm. If its original length was 1 meter and cross-sectional area is 1 cm^2, what is the Young's modulus (E) of the material?
A) 2.5 × 10^9 Pa
B) 5 × 10^9 Pa
C) 7.5 × 10^9 Pa
D) 1 × 10^10 Pa
Correct Answer: B

Solution:
Young's modulus E is defined as stress divided by strain, where stress = force / area and strain = extension / original length. Stress = (500 N) / (1 cm^2) = 5 × 10^6 Pa, and strain = (2 mm) / (1 m) = 2 × 10^-3. E = stress / strain = (5 × 10^6 Pa) / (2 × 10^-3) = 2.5 × 10^9 Pa.

Explanation:
This question tests understanding of Young's modulus and its calculation from stress and strain values.

Common mistake note:
Common mistake: Using the wrong units for force, area, or extension; not converting lengths to consistent units before calculating strain.

Problem 6 (Control system stability):
A closed-loop control system has a transfer function G(s) = K / (s + 2)(s + 3). What is the range of K values that ensure the system is stable?
A) K > 0
B) K < 6
C) K < 5
D) K < 4
Correct Answer: D

Solution:
For a control system to be stable, all poles of its transfer function must have negative real parts. The poles are the solutions to (s + 2)(s + 3) = 0, which gives s = -2 and s = -3. To ensure stability, K / (s + 2)(s + 3) must not introduce any additional poles with positive real parts. For a numerator of degree 1, this means the magnitude of K must be less than the product of the magnitudes of the denominator's pole locations: |K| < |(-2) * (-3)| = 6. Thus, K < 4.

Explanation:
This question tests understanding of control system stability and pole-zero placement.

Common mistake note:
Common mistake: Assuming that any positive K value ensures stability or not considering the effect of the numerator on pole locations.

Problem 7 (Thermodynamic cycle efficiency):
A heat engine operates between two temperature reservoirs at 600 K and 300 K. The engine absorbs 10 kJ of heat from the hot reservoir and releases 6 kJ to the cold reservoir during each cycle. What is the Carnot efficiency of this engine?
A) 0.2
B) 0.4
C) 0.5
D) 0.6
Correct Answer: C

Solution:
The Carnot efficiency (η_c) is given by 1 - T_c / T_h, where T_c is the temperature of the cold reservoir and T_h is the temperature of the hot reservoir. η_c = 1 - (300 K) / (600 K) = 0.5.

Explanation:
This question tests understanding of Carnot efficiency and its application to thermodynamic cycles.

Common mistake note:
Common mistake: Using the actual heat absorbed or released by the engine instead of considering the ideal case represented by Carnot efficiency.
